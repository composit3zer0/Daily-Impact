Dhamma Corner

In periods of intense global turbulence—where financial markets swing wildly, critical infrastructure falters overnight, and geopolitical tensions choke vital trade arteries—the human mind instinctively seeks refuge in control. We calculate capital expenditure across continents, monitor semiconductor supply chains, and build elaborate fortresses of security, hoping to insulate ourselves from uncertainty. Yet early Buddhist psychology points to a fundamental flaw in this enterprise: the external world is inherently unstable, marked by constant change, friction, and unsatisfactoriness (*dukkha*).

The Buddha frequently reminded practitioners that trying to force the conditioned world to conform to an unyielding expectation of permanence is the root cause of mental suffering. When air traffic networks collapse or regional disputes threaten energy supplies, we witness macro-level manifestations of impermanence (*anicca*). For the modern leader, innovator, or digital nomad, true resilience does not stem from mastering every external variable—an impossible ambition—but from cultivating an internal anchor that remains unshakeable amidst shifting conditions.

In the Bhayabherava Sutta, the Buddha details how he examined his own mind to conquer fear and dread. He realized that terror and anxiety arise when one is dependent upon external circumstances for stability. By developing clear comprehension (*sati-sampajañña*) and training the mind to observe sensory and mental impressions without immediate reactivity, one stops fighting the current of reality. When you see market volatility, technological disruption, or logistical failure not as personal catastrophes but as natural phenomena arising and passing away, fear dissolves. Equanimity (*upekkha*) becomes your ultimate strategic asset—allowing you to respond with clear, compassionate precision rather than frantic agitation.

Reference: MN 4: Bhayabherava Sutta (Discourse on Fear and Dread)
