Dhamma Corner

In periods of intense global disruption—when monetary authorities scramble to stabilize currencies, supply chains fracture under geopolitical friction, and enterprise leaders race to harness artificial intelligence—the human mind is perpetually caught between two reactive extremes: frantic agitation and fearful clinging. We watch the ticker tapes, audit our balance sheets, and calculate our exposure to unseen shocks, mistaking nervous mental motion for effective strategy.

Early Buddhist psychology identifies this turbulence not merely as a feature of external markets, but as the direct outcome of conditioned craving (tanha) and ignorance (avijja). When stability is perceived as fragile, the unexamined mind tightens its grip, attempting to control the uncontrollable. Yet, the foundational teaching of impermanence (anicca) demonstrates that attempting to freeze a shifting world into a permanent state of security is like trying to catch water in a fist; the harder you squeeze, the faster it slips away.

For leaders, innovators, and modern nomads navigating rapid transitions, true clarity does not arise from predicting every macro twist or outrunning geopolitical volatility. It emerges from cultivating a grounded, unshakeable awareness that observes conditions as they arise without identifying with their turbulence. By establishing mindfulness (sati) as a stable anchor, you learn to act with precision, compassion, and strategic detachment. You execute necessary operational changes without internal panic, recognizing that unskillful reactivity only compounds suffering, whereas serene discernment meets every crisis with poise and clear vision.

Reference: SN 35.28: Ādittapariyāya Sutta (The Fire Sermon) — Expounding how the unexamined senses and external phenomena burn with the fires of greed, hatred, and delusion, and how the noble disciple develops disenchantment and release through radical non-attachment.
