Dhamma Corner

This week the markets rose by a record margin, and bitcoin fell. Warships disabled a freighter, and a chipmaker's name was tied to a data center it says it did not supply. In a week like this, the mind swings hard between hope and dread. Early Buddhist teaching offers a steady way to look at that swing.

In the Lokadhamma Sutta (AN 8.6), the Buddha names eight worldly conditions that turn the world: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. The sutta says these conditions follow the world and the world follows them. They are ordinary and unavoidable. They are also impermanent, changing in nature.

The sutta then contrasts two kinds of people. The uninstructed person welcomes the pleasant conditions and resists the unpleasant ones, and so is pushed around by every one. He is excited by a week of gains and shaken by a week of losses. The instructed disciple sees that these conditions are impermanent, unsatisfying and subject to change, and so is not carried off by them. That is not indifference. It is a settled way of looking, and it lets you respond clearly.

For a leader or investor, the practical point is plain. Gain and loss will arrive, and so will praise and blame. A portfolio that doubles an index's best week since 2022 and then gives it back does not change who you are. A hearing in Congress that brings blame to a company or a leader does not decide what is true. If your steadiness depends on these eight conditions, you have handed the controls to the world.

For innovators and digital nomads, the same teaching applies to a different kind of swing. Plans change, routes close, and a visa or a flight can vanish. The sutta does not ask you to stop planning. It asks you to notice how quickly the mind grasps at the pleasant outcome and recoils from the unpleasant one, and to see that this grasping is where the suffering sits.

The practice is simple to state and hard to do. When a headline lifts you, note "pleasure, arising, passing." When one frightens you, note "pain, arising, passing." Then act, with the same care in both cases. Good decisions are made from that evenness, and the sutta presents it as a path toward freedom from suffering.

Try this today. Pick the one development that moved you most, whether it was a price, a headline or a message. Name which of the eight conditions it touches. Then ask what you would do if you were not trying to hold on to it or push it away.

Scientists discover hidden brain changes linked to common sleep disorders
Source: ScienceDaily · Published: 8 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 4 minutes
Summary: Sleep disorders may leave a physical imprint on the brain, according to a meta-analysis from Florida International University that pooled 57 brain-imaging studies of adults. Rather than examining one condition at a time, the researchers compared dyssomnias such as insomnia and sleep apnea with parasomnias such as sleepwalking, nightmare disorder and sleep terrors. Both groups were associated with reduced thalamus structure, notably the pulvinar, which helps direct attention and cognitive control. Parasomnias also showed changes in the posterior cingulate cortex, linked to motivation and emotional regulation. The findings may help explain slower reactions and poorer decisions in affected people, and could eventually inform diagnosis. The evidence is associational, so causation remains unproven.

How trauma and PTSD affect refugees’ lives after migration
Source: The Mental Elf · Published: 24 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 7 minutes
Summary: Trauma does not end at the border. A systematic review of 38 studies, covering 15,394 adult refugees and asylum seekers, asked how trauma and PTSD affect social functioning after migration, something no review had examined before. Social relationships were the most studied area and mostly showed harm, including mistrust, isolation, shame and withdrawal; integration and employment also suffered, while findings on everyday functioning were mixed.
