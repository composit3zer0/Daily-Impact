Dhamma Corner

Yesterday's markets offered a clear lesson in how quickly fortunes turn. The Dow crossed 50,000 while the Nasdaq fell nearly one and a half percent. Gold lost almost three percent in a day, and bitcoin slid below $60,000. Meanwhile, Sanofi announced a $41 billion purchase, a triumph for one boardroom and a shock for another. Early Buddhist teaching has a precise name for this pattern.

In the Lokadhamma Sutta, the Buddha, as recorded in the Anguttara Nikaya, describes eight worldly conditions that keep the world turning: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. The sutta says that these conditions follow the world, and the world follows them. They arise for everyone, and they are impermanent, unsatisfying, and subject to change.

The sutta then draws a distinction that matters for anyone whose work involves risk. The uninstructed ordinary person is elated by gain and fame, and dismayed by loss and disrepute. Such a person is entangled in attraction and repulsion, and is not freed from suffering. The instructed disciple of the noble ones also meets all eight conditions, yet understands them as they are. Gain and loss still come, but they do not take over the mind.

This is not a call to indifference or passivity. A founder still has to hire, an investor still has to size a position, and a nomad still has to renew a visa. The teaching points to something more practical: the difference between acting on events and being pushed around by them. When gold falls, you can review your holdings with a steady mind. When an acquisition lifts your stock, you can enjoy it without building your identity on it.

Notice also the sutta's emphasis on noticing. The practice begins by recognising: this is gain, this is loss, this will change. That simple naming, repeated daily, creates a small space between stimulus and reaction. In that space, a leader can ask better questions. What do I actually know? What is fear telling me? What would I do if I trusted that this too would pass?

For those who move between countries, industries, and income streams, impermanence is not abstract. Rules change in ninety days, as the new chip licensing regime shows. Partnerships shift, as Japan's public mood towards China shows. A practitioner of this teaching holds plans lightly, prepares sincerely, and keeps their sense of worth in conduct rather than in outcomes.

Today, when a number moves against you, try this: pause, name the condition, and breathe. Then act as wisely as you can.

Personal Reflection

When the numbers fall, it is easy to feel that you have fallen with them. Yesterday some people gained and some lost, and most of it had little to do with who they are. Notice how much of your sense of worth you have quietly attached to things that move every day: a balance, a title, someone's approval. You do not have to give any of them up. You only have to see them clearly. What would remain steady in you if all of them shifted at once?

What part of your sense of self would still be here tomorrow, whatever the numbers do today?
