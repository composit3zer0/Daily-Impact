Dhamma Corner

This week gave us a lesson in how quickly fortunes turn. Last week the Nasdaq fell ten percent in its worst stretch since 2020. On Monday a chief executive was reinstated, his company's shares rose more than thirty percent in a day, and the index rebounded sharply. Oil slid, gold climbed, bitcoin wobbled around sixty thousand. The early teachings have a plain word for this: aniccā, impermanence. Conditions arise and pass, and the mind that clings to either the peak or the trough is the one that suffers.

The Sallatha Sutta, the Discourse on the Arrow, gives a precise tool for such days. In it the Buddha describes how an untrained person, touched by painful feeling, sorrows and laments and is distraught. That person feels two pains, like someone struck by one arrow and then by a second. The first arrow is the event itself: the loss, the headline, the drawdown. The second arrow is what we add: the panic, the resentment, the rehearsal of what might have been. The trained disciple, the sutta says, feels the first arrow only. Pain is not denied. The mind simply does not pile its own wound on top.

For a leader or investor this is practical, not abstract. A falling position will hurt, and a missed rally will sting. The teaching asks that you notice the feeling as feeling. Is it painful, pleasant or neutral? Then see whether craving or aversion is attached to it. The sutta also observes that one who lacks any other way of finding ease turns to sensual pleasure, here the endless refresh of the price screen, which only strengthens the habit. A person with training sees a different path: seeing the feeling clearly, without being pulled into reaction.

For the traveller and digital nomad the same applies. Visas shift, flights are cancelled, currencies swing, and a Gulf conflict can reprice a fuel bill overnight. These are first arrows. The question each morning is what second arrow you are about to fire. One measure of practice is how quickly you notice it.

Nothing here counsels passivity. Elsewhere the early texts praise diligence, prudence and right livelihood. The point is that clear action comes from a steady mind, and a steady mind begins by seeing the arrow for what it is. Let today's volatility be a reminder rather than a verdict: what rose can fall, what fell can rise, and your peace need not wait on either.
