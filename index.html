Dhamma Corner

In moments of rapid industrial expansion, geopolitical tension, and shifting financial tides, human consciousness easily becomes entangled in external volatility. We scan the news for signals of safety, calculating risks in supply chains, semiconductor yields, and energy chokepoints as though absolute security could be engineered out of raw matter. Yet the early Buddhist teachings repeatedly remind us that external conditions are inherently unstable, characterized by constant change, unsatisfactoriness, and the absence of a permanent core.

When the Buddha addressed the nature of worldly phenomena, he pointed directly to the illusion of control. Leaders, innovators, and strategists invest immense intellectual capital into building resilient systems—superfactories, diversified trade routes, and automated workflows—hoping to insulate themselves against disruption. While practical vigilance is necessary for effective stewardship, clinging to these formations as permanent sources of security breeds deep anxiety. True stability is not found in manipulating external variables until they cease to fluctuate, but in cultivating an unshakeable inner composure that remains clear amidst turbulence.

The practice for the modern decision-maker begins with recognizing the conditioned nature of all phenomena. Every market surge, every geopolitical concession, and every technological breakthrough arises due to dependent origination—conditions meeting and dissolving in continuous flux. By observing the mind's reactive urge to grasp at favorable outcomes and push away adverse shocks, one develops discernment. This clear seeing allows you to act decisively in the marketplace and executive suite without binding your inner peace to outcomes beyond your control. In stillness, right action arises naturally, unclouded by panic or greed.

Related discourse: Anatta-lakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59) — On the impermanent, unsatisfactory, and non-self nature of all conditioned formations.
