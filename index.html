Dhamma Corner

Today's headlines carry a common thread: sudden loss and sudden gain. Bitcoin touches a four-month low, the Dow drops more than a thousand points' worth of sentiment in a day, a chief executive's departure shakes confidence in a company, and a record offering prices above its range. Leaders and investors feel each swing as if it were a verdict on themselves.

The early teachings address this directly. In the Sallatha Sutta, the Arrow, the Buddha describes how an untrained person, touched by painful feeling, suffers twice. The first arrow is the painful event itself. The second arrow is the sorrow, resentment and bewilderment that the mind adds on top. By contrast, the trained disciple feels the first arrow but does not shoot the second.

This is practical guidance for a volatile week. A falling portfolio is a painful feeling, a real loss and a real tightening of options. The second arrow is the story that follows: "I should have known," "they ruined me," "this will never recover." That narrative drives panic selling, rash hiring and firing decisions, and sleepless nights. The sutta, as paraphrased here, points out that pleasant and neutral feelings are also met without wisdom by the untrained. The one who craves pleasure when pain arrives is simply reaching for the next arrow.

For an investor, this means separating the data from the drama. Note the price, note the risk, and then ask what action the facts actually support. For a founder facing a leadership crisis, it means acting on the board's investigation and the customer's needs rather than on the mood in the room. For a digital nomad whose income sits on shifting exchange rates, it means preparing sensibly and then letting the rest go.

The sutta also describes a deeper freedom: the disciple understands the arising and passing of feeling and is no longer pulled by it. This is not indifference to loss. It is the capacity to take a loss cleanly, with attention and without adding cruelty to oneself. Compassion belongs here too. When a leader falls, as it did for the Nvidia founder this month, we need not feast on the story. Everyone is subject to change, and today's winner is not exempt.

A simple practice for today: when a number on a screen moves against you, pause for three breaths. Name the feeling as it is, such as tightness, heat or dread. Then ask whether the next action comes from clear seeing or from the second arrow. Decide only after that.

Millions take fish oil for brain health. A two-year study found no benefit
Source: ScienceDaily · Published: 22 Oct 2026 · Reading time: 6 minutes
Summary: A two-year randomized trial at Keck Medicine of USC tested whether high-dose fish oil protects the ageing brain, and found that it does not. The study enrolled 365 adults aged 55 to 80 who rarely ate fish and were at elevated Alzheimer's risk, nearly half carrying the APOE4 variant. Half received 2,000 mg of DHA daily, half a placebo. Cerebrospinal fluid DHA rose by an average of 17%, so the nutrient reached the brain, yet memory, thinking tests and hippocampal shrinkage were no better than with placebo. For the many people buying omega-3 supplements for cognition, the result is sobering, though it applies to this group of older adults; researchers are now investigating how the brain uses these fats.

Personal Reflection

When a number on a screen falls, or a leader you admired stumbles, notice how quickly your own sense of safety rises or sinks with it. Much of what feels like your identity may be borrowed from outcomes you do not control: a valuation, a title, a reputation. Today, take a quiet minute before you check anything.
