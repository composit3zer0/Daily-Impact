Dhamma Corner

In periods of intense global volatility—where legislative stalemates over war powers, multi-billion-dollar defense expenditures, and market corrections keep modern leaders on edge—the human mind instinctively seeks refuge in control. We scrutinize macro indicators, forecast semiconductor yields, and attempt to engineer absolute security in an inherently unstable environment. Yet, early Buddhist psychology reveals that the root of suffering does not lie in the external turbulence of the world, but in the mind's compulsive demand for permanence where impermanence reigns.

When the Buddha addressed monks and lay leaders alike, he consistently pointed toward the mechanism of clinging. Leaders navigating high-stakes corporate or geopolitical transitions often fall into the trap of identification, treating transient market outcomes and shifting alliances as definitive proof of their success or failure. This mental reactivity clouds strategic clarity. By cultivating radical equanimity—observing the rise and fall of economic cycles, geopolitical tensions, and technological paradigms without internal agitation—one develops the unshakeable poise required for truly discerning leadership.

True sovereignty is not found in dominating external conditions, which are forever subject to decay, but in mastering the internal landscape of desire and aversion. When you perceive that every conditioned phenomenon is marked by change, anxiety subsides, replaced by a clear, responsive awareness that meets each changing condition with measured, compassionate intelligence.

Reference: SN 35.28: Ādittapariyāya Sutta (The Fire Sermon) — An exposition on how the mind burns with the fires of attachment, aversion, and delusion when sensory and worldly phenomena are misapprehended as permanent.
