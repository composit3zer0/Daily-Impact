Dhamma Corner

Today's headlines share one feature: certainty that does not last. A coalition mandate with a 28-day clock. A truce with an expiry date. An oil price that swings on each diplomatic rumour. Anyone making decisions in this environment is pulled toward two errors, which are to believe the loudest account or to trust nothing at all.

The Kalama Sutta, in the Anguttara Nikaya, addresses this. The Kalamas, villagers visited by many teachers who contradicted each other, did not know whom to trust. The sutta records the Buddha advising them not to accept a claim merely because of tradition, repute, scripture, logic, appearance, or the standing of the speaker. Instead, they were to see for themselves which things are unskillful and lead to harm and suffering, and which are skillful and lead to welfare and happiness. Paraphrased, the test is practical: what increases greed, hatred and delusion, and what reduces them?

For an investor, this means asking whether a thesis rests on evidence you have checked or on the confident tone of a headline. Greed inflates a position when prices run, and fear dumps it when they fall. The sutta suggests watching these movements in your own mind before acting on them. A decision made from clarity differs from one made from craving, even when the trade looks identical.

For a founder, a policymaker, or a traveller crossing borders on short notice, the same discipline applies. When a ceasefire frays or a trade truce lapses, the mind reaches for a story that ends the discomfort. The early teachings counsel patience with not-knowing. You can prepare, hold contingencies and act, and still accept that outcomes are not yours to command.

The sutta also points to the wholesome roots of non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion. In practice, this could mean negotiating firmly without contempt for the counterpart, building a supply chain without hoarding, and sharing information honestly even when silence would pay. These are small choices, and they accumulate into a character.

Let today be a day of checking: verify the claim, notice the craving, and then choose the action that leaves you and others with less harm.

3. Psychology, Psychiatry, and Mental Health
Cognitive restructuring in PTSD: exploring why negative beliefs can be so sticky
Source: The Mental Elf · Published: 27 Oct 2026 · Reading time: 8 minutes
Summary: Why do negative beliefs about a trauma stay stuck even when therapy challenges them? Australian researchers put 70 adults with PTSD and 66 trauma-exposed controls through cognitive restructuring while undergoing 7-Tesla brain scanning, then modelled how a thalamocortical network centred on the mediodorsal thalamus and medial prefrontal cortex interacted. People with PTSD endorsed negative statements far more strongly beforehand, yet reduced their endorsement of challenged statements just as much as controls. The authors propose the prefrontal cortex acts as a gatekeeper that devalues self-relevant negative beliefs. Important limits: the design was cross-sectional, ratings were immediate, and nothing shows durable change or real-world therapy benefit. It is useful for understanding why roughly a third still meet diagnosis after treatment.

Personal Reflection

Deadlines, expiries and mandates fill the news, yet your own life runs on quieter clocks. You may be waiting for a sign, a deal, or a clear moment before you decide something that matters to you. Often the uncertainty is not out there. It is the discomfort of not yet knowing what you want. Notice which of your choices today are being made to escape that feeling, and which come from something steadier.

What decision are you postponing in the hope that certainty will arrive, and what would you choose if it never did?
