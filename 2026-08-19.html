Dhamma Corner

In an era defined by accelerating corporate restructuring, shifting geopolitical trade routes, and the unsettling autonomy of artificial intelligence systems, human beings find themselves frantically grasping for stability in a world constructed of shifting sand. We build elaborate enterprises, hedge against algorithmic obsolescence, and attempt to secure our positions within volatile global hierarchies, only to discover that every external shelter is inherently impermanent.

The Buddha addressed this very delusion of permanence not through political maneuvering or technological containment, but by directing investigation inward toward the mechanics of conditioned existence. When the mind perceives external phenomena—whether fluctuating market prices, geopolitical conflicts, or software code executing beyond human oversight—it habitually reacts with craving or aversion, generating unnecessary suffering.

True intelligence, according to the early teachings, lies in discerning the three marks of existence across all phenomena: impermanence (anicca), unsatisfactoriness (dukkha), and non-self (anatta). When leaders, innovators, and modern digital nomads anchor their awareness in mindfulness rather than reactive anxiety, they perceive that systems rise and fall according to natural causal laws. By relinquishing the compulsive urge to control what cannot be permanently held, the mind discovers an unshakable refuge untouched by external turbulence.

Reference: Anatta-lakkhana Sutta (SN 22.59) — On the impermanent, unsatisfactory, and non-self nature of all conditioned formations.
