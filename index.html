Dhamma Corner

On a day when a stock index fell nearly five percent and recovered within hours, the oldest question in the Pali canon becomes practical: how does a mind meet painful news without adding a second wound?

In the Sallatha Sutta, the Buddha describes how an untrained person, when touched by painful feeling, sorrows, grieves and laments. He compares this to being struck by one arrow and then a second. The trained disciple feels the first arrow, which is the physical or circumstantial blow, but does not shoot the second arrow of mental anguish, resentment and panic. The sutta traces how pleasant feeling leads the untrained to cling and unpleasant feeling to aversion, so that the mind is bound in both directions.

Consider the headlines. Tankers burn in the Strait of Hormuz. Bitcoin falls by a quarter in a month. Gold slips to a fourteen-month low. These are first arrows: real losses, real risks, and often beyond anyone's control. The second arrow is the one we fire ourselves: the compulsive refresh of the price screen, the midnight rewriting of the plan, the story that everything is lost or that we should have known. Leaders and investors often mistake that second arrow for diligence.

The sutta does not recommend numbness. It recommends clear knowing. The disciple knows the feeling as feeling, notes its arising and passing, and is not enslaved by it. For someone managing capital, a team or a business in another country, this means deliberately separating information from reaction. Read the data. Name the fear as fear. Decide only after the body has settled.

A second teaching in the same discourse is that sensual pleasure is not the only refuge from pain. Those who know only pleasure as an escape hasten to it whenever discomfort arises. Today's markets reward this reflex: the quick purchase of the dip, the quick trade into comfort. The practice of the path is to grow a refuge that does not depend on price: ethical conduct, steady attention and a clear intention. That refuge is portable, which suits those who live between countries.

Practically: before acting on today's news, pause for three breaths. Ask what has actually changed in your obligations and your time horizon. If nothing has, let the first arrow be only the first. If something has, act from clarity rather than fright. Impermanence cuts both ways: the plunge will pass, and so will the rebound.

7. Parenting and Special Needs
What Children Really Notice About Honesty
Source: Psychology Today · Published: 25 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 4 minutes
Summary: Children watch what parents do more closely than they listen to what parents say. Psychologist Victoria Talwar and colleagues told stories to 127 children aged 5 to 12 in which a parent either practised the honesty they preached or contradicted it. Children noticed the mismatch, viewed consistent parents more positively, and judged truthful parents as more trustworthy than parents who lied, whatever the reason for the lie. When forced to choose between words and actions as a guide to what another child would learn, they often weighted behaviour more. The study used hypothetical stories rather than real families, but its message is practical: modelling honesty, including owning mistakes, matters.
Personal Reflection

Today's screens swung from panic to relief in a single afternoon. Notice that the world did not change nearly as much as your sense of safety did. You have probably felt this in your own life too: a message, a number, a rumor, and suddenly your whole future looks different. What you reach for in those moments reveals what you believe will keep you whole. It may be money, status, a plan, or someone's approval. None of these is wrong, but each is borrowed.

When the ground shakes, what are you trying to protect: the thing itself, or your picture of who you are without it?
