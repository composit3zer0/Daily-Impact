Dhamma Corner

Yields climb, a coin loses a tenth of its value in a fortnight, a strait stays shut, a famous founder walks out. On such mornings the mind does two things at once: it registers the facts, and it adds a second layer of dread. Early Buddhist teaching separates these carefully.

In the Sallatha Sutta, "The Arrow" (SN 36.6), the Buddha is described as comparing painful experience to being struck by an arrow. An untaught person, struck once, then is struck by a second arrow: the grieving, resentment and agitation that follow. The trained disciple feels only the first. The loss in your portfolio, the cancelled flight or the broken deal is the first arrow. The spiral of "I should have known," or "this ruins everything," is the second, and that one is shot by the mind itself.

The sutta also observes that the untaught person, when contact with pain arises, seeks delight in sensual pleasure, because they know no other escape. For a leader, this has a modern face: when markets sour, we reach for distraction, a new trade, a scroll through feeds, a fresh project, anything to avoid sitting with uncertainty. The teaching points elsewhere. It describes understanding feelings as they are, as pleasant, painful or neutral, without being bound to them.

Consider how this applies today. Nobody can control the Federal Reserve, the Strait of Hormuz or the next leadership announcement. What remains is the quality of one's response. Read the headlines as information. Name the feeling that arises: anxiety, envy, excitement. Notice that it is also impermanent. Prices, alliances and valuations all arise and pass, and the tradition suggests that clinging to any of them as a refuge is the root of suffering.

This is not passivity. A person who does not add the second arrow can think more clearly: rebalance, hedge, renegotiate, or simply wait. Equanimity is the condition for good decisions, not a replacement for them. For the digital nomad whose life sits on borders, visas and flight routes, and for the investor whose wealth sits on screens, the practice is the same: feel the first arrow fully, and decline the second.

Try this today. When a notification stings, pause for three breaths and ask: what is the actual event, and what is the story I am adding?

Yale scientists reveal what really slows aging and what doesn't
Source: ScienceDaily · Published: 14 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 6 minutes
Summary: Aging may be more malleable than once thought, and a Yale team has tried to sort which interventions actually move the needle. Pooling 51 clinical studies and more than 110 DNA methylation biomarkers, including 16 major epigenetic clocks, they compared biological age before and after interventions across prescription drugs, lifestyle changes, over-the-counter supplements and medical procedures. Certain medications and lifestyle changes, notably regular exercise combined with a healthy eating plan, produced measurable reductions, while popular supplements generally did not. The key caveat, stressed by the researchers, is that shifts in these markers do not necessarily mean longer life or less age-related disease. It is a useful corrective to anti-aging marketing.

7. Parenting and Special Needs
A surprising brain difference may explain why some kids struggle with math
Source: ScienceDaily · Published: 12 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 4 minutes
Summary: Why do some children stay stuck on maths despite practice and instruction? A Stanford study of 68 children aged 8 to 10 suggests part of the answer lies in strategy selection. Children with developmental dyscalculia were less effective at using varied strategies, slower to switch methods, less responsive when problem difficulty changed, and did not improve at choosing better approaches over time. Activity in a particular brain system differed and predicted how effectively they counted and switched. The findings point to attention, memory and cognitive flexibility, not only number knowledge. The authors caution that the links do not prove causation and that difficulties vary widely between children, which matters for parents and teachers seeking tailored support.
Personal Reflection

When a number drops, you may notice how quickly your sense of worth tries to follow it. Prices, titles and valuations are loud, and they borrow a lot of your attention. Yet most of what actually steadies you was never listed on any exchange: the people who would answer your call, the work you would do unpaid, the way you treat others when nothing is at stake. Today, watch which of your worries are about the world, and which are about who you think you must be in it.

If every number you track went quiet for a week, what would you still be sure you are?
