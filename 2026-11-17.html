Dhamma Corner

This week the world has handed us a textbook demonstration of instability. Oil has fallen by roughly a third from its peak. Stablecoin supply has shrunk to a year-to-date low. Three powerful leaders are preparing to meet, and no one can say with confidence what will come of it. Each headline invites a strong emotional response: elation, dread, a rush to act.

The early teachings address precisely this. In the Lokavipatti or "Worldly Conditions" discourse of the Anguttara Nikaya (AN 8.6), the Buddha describes eight conditions that keep the world turning: gain and loss, fame and disrepute, praise and blame, pleasure and pain. The discourse says that the instructed noble disciple reflects that these conditions are impermanent, unsatisfactory and subject to change. Such a person is not elated by the pleasant ones, nor distressed by the unpleasant ones.

Notice what the sutta does not say. It does not tell us to withdraw from markets, politics or ambition. It does not claim that gain and loss are unreal. It says that they arise and pass, and that the mind which clings to one side and recoils from the other suffers needlessly. The teaching is a skill of response, not an instruction to disengage.

For a leader or investor, the practice is concrete. When a position gains, notice the wish for it to continue. When it loses, notice the aversion and the urge to blame. Both are movements of the mind that can be seen before they are acted upon. A decision made after that pause tends to be quieter and more accurate than one made inside the surge. For a digital nomad whose income, visas and location can shift on a policy announcement, the same principle applies: hold plans lightly, and keep your sense of worth separate from your circumstances.

There is also a compassionate dimension. Falling oil prices relieve some households and burden others; a tense summit weighs on people who have no seat at the table. The wise response to impermanence is not coldness but care. Because nothing stays as it is, the suffering of today can ease and the comfort of today can end. That recognition tends to soften judgement of others and loosen the grip on our own gains.

Today, when a headline moves you strongly, take three breaths and name the condition at work: gain, loss, praise, blame, pleasure or pain. Then ask what the situation actually requires.

Source: Psychology Today · Published: 16 Nov 2026 · Reading time: 7 minutes
Summary: Whenever nonspeaking autistic people who spell on letterboards make headlines, critics invoke Clever Hans, the horse that seemed to do arithmetic but was reading cues from questioners. This author, drawing on published studies and the author's own observations, argues the analogy fails. Hans needed only a stop signal, whereas spelling demands choosing among 26 letters repeatedly.
