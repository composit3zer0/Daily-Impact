Dhamma Corner

When sudden calamities shake the earth—such as the devastating Himalayan glacial collapse reported this week—the human mind instinctively scrambles for permanence in a world governed by flux. We build defenses, establish trade barriers, and draft policies hoping to arrest the relentless march of change. Yet early Buddhist teaching reminds us that resistance to the fundamental nature of reality is the direct root of suffering.

In the Sela Sutta, the Buddha emphasizes that the world is swept away, leading neither to shelter nor protection for those who cling to conditioned phenomena as permanent. Leaders, innovators, and strategists often fall into the trap of believing that intellectual mastery or capital accumulation can insulate them from systemic fragility. True stability does not come from controlling external volatility, but from cultivating an unshakeable inner composure that understands the conditioned nature of all things.

When market structures fracture, diplomatic ties fray, and environmental crises unfold, the practitioner applies clear comprehension. By seeing the arising and passing of formations without grasping, one acts with fierce compassion and strategic clarity unclouded by panic. Action taken from a foundation of inner stillness is infinitely more potent than reaction driven by fear.

Reference: Sutta Nipāta 3.7 (Sela Sutta)
