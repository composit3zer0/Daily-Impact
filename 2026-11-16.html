Dhamma Corner

Today's headlines arrive as a flood: mined straits, carriers on the move, borders closing, markets leaning on a single engine. Each report invites a reaction before it invites understanding. The early teachings offer a discipline for this moment, and it begins with how we receive what we are told.

In the Kesamutti discourse, often called the Kalama Sutta, the Buddha meets villagers confused by competing teachers. According to the sutta, he advises them not to rely on hearsay, tradition, rumor, scriptural authority, logic alone, or a speaker's charisma. Instead they are to look at the states themselves. Do greed, hatred, and delusion, when present, lead to harm and suffering? Do their absence lead to welfare and happiness? The test is lived and ethical, not merely informational.

For an investor, this is a practical filter. Many of today's claims are unconfirmed, such as the Guard's tanker report or the details of a third carrier. The sutta does not tell us to be cynical. It asks us to notice what is arising in us as we read. Is it greed, the urge to chase a move before the facts are in? Is it aversion, the hardening against a country or bloc? Is it delusion, the belief that one engine, one asset, or one alliance will hold forever?

For a leader, the same teaching applies to decisions. A strategy built on fear of loss tends to produce harm, as does one built on appetite for more. The sutta points instead toward a mind free of those three roots, a mind that can act with clarity because it is not being driven. Such a mind can hedge, diversify, and negotiate without panic.

For the digital nomad, impermanence is the daily condition: borders, flights, currencies, and platforms can change in a morning. The discourse also describes the practitioner dwelling with a heart of loving-kindness, compassion, sympathetic joy, and equanimity, pervading every direction. That is not a retreat from the world's problems. It is the ground from which we can respond to them wisely, remembering that behind each headline are people who also wish to be free of suffering.

Before the next click, pause. Ask what the story is stirring in you, and whether acting on it would lead to your welfare and the welfare of others. That small inquiry is the sutta's invitation.
