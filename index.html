Dhamma Corner

Today's headlines carry a common theme: the hope for something permanent. A president pledges a "permanent" settlement with Iran. A climate conference spends two extra days trying to fix a text, then closes with none. Peacekeepers hand over posts and nobody can say yet whether the handover will hold. Underneath each story is the old human wish for solid ground.

Early Buddhist teaching does not scold that wish. It examines it. In the suttas, the Buddha's first teaching describes dukkha, the unsatisfactoriness that arises when we cling to what is impermanent as if it were secure. Agreements, valuations, alliances and even a well-built career are conditioned things. They arise with causes and pass away when those causes change. A leader who sees this clearly is not made cynical. He is made sober.

For decision-makers, that sobriety has practical shape. Plan for the deal that holds and for the deal that does not. Keep reserves, keep options, and refuse to let a single announcement govern your sense of safety. An investor watching the Nasdaq's weeks of losses, or an operator watching Hormuz, can notice the craving to be told that all will be well. That craving is where mistakes begin.

The Kalama Sutta offers a second lesson for a day of conflicting claims. The Kalamas, visited by many teachers who each praised their own views, were advised by the Buddha, as the sutta records, not to accept a teaching merely because of tradition, report, authority or the speaker's charm. They were encouraged to look at what the teaching actually produces. Do greed, hatred and delusion grow, or do they fall away? Do the results lead to harm or to welfare? This is a useful test for every pledge and press release.

Apply it to your own information diet this morning. Which headlines inflamed you? Which clarified? A digital nomad moving between jurisdictions, or a founder facing a volatile market, needs steadiness more than speed. The sutta points toward a mind that is clear, kind and not easily pushed around by either hope or fear.

One practice for today: before reacting to any major news, pause for three breaths and ask what greed, aversion or delusion might be shaping your response. Then act, carefully, with compassion for everyone affected, including the farmer downstream and the soldier at the checkpoint.

Personal Reflection

Many of today's stories turn on a promise of something lasting: a deal, a pact, a handover. You make your own versions of these. A plan for the year, a role you hold, a relationship you assume will stay as it is. Notice how much of your calm depends on things continuing exactly as they are. That is not weakness; it is human. But it is worth seeing clearly, because most of what steadies you today may change before the year ends.

If the thing you are most sure will last turned out not to, what in you would still be standing?
