Dhamma Corner

In periods of intense global volatility—when geopolitical chokepoints close, economic forecasts shudder, and natural disasters upend communities—the human mind instinctively seeks refuge in control. We scrutinize ticker symbols, stockpile provisions, and draft intricate contingency plans, hoping to construct an unassailable fortress against uncertainty. Yet the early Buddhist teachings repeatedly remind us that attempting to secure permanent stability within an inherently unstable realm is like trying to nail water to a wall.

The Buddha addressed this directly in the Sallatha Sutta, pointing out that untrained minds experience the unpredictable shocks of existence as a double wounding. When an unfavorable event occurs—be it a stalled diplomatic pact, a sudden market correction, or a flash flood—we not only feel the physical or situational impact, but we immediately create a secondary layer of mental reactivity: anger, anxiety, grief, and frantic resistance. We treat impermanence as a personal insult rather than a universal law of nature.

For leaders, innovators, and strategists navigating rapid structural change, the Dhamma offers a vital paradigm shift. Equanimity (upekkha) is not passive indifference or reckless resignation; it is the clear-eyed recognition of conditions as they are, unclouded by panic or craving. When you perceive that economic cycles, political shifts, and physical elements arise and pass away according to conditions, you stop fighting reality and start responding with precision. By anchoring your awareness in the present moment rather than projecting catastrophic narratives into an unknown future, you preserve the inner stillness required to make sound, compassionate decisions when everyone else is reacting in fear.

Reference: SN 36.6: Sallatha Sutta (The Arrow)
Personal Reflection

We spend immense quantities of energy attempting to orchestrate the external environment—managing our portfolios, securing our logistics, and polishing our professional standing—as if absolute security were merely a matter of better planning. Yet beneath the frantic drive for control often lies a quiet, unexamined fear of vulnerability.

When you strip away your titles, your strategies, and your daily output, what remains? Notice the subtle tension in your chest when things do not go according to script, and ask yourself whether your striving is designed to solve a genuine problem or simply to numb the discomfort of uncertainty.

What would change in your decisions today if you stopped treating uncertainty as an enemy to be conquered and started treating it as the very space in which you live?
