Dhamma Corner

This week the world watched a record share offering priced above its range, three nuclear powers declare that war cannot be won, and tankers burning in a narrow strait. Each of these arrives with a story attached. Each story asks us to believe quickly: that a new machine will redeem us, that a statement will keep us safe, that fear is the only sensible response.

The Kalama Sutta, from the Anguttara Nikaya, speaks to exactly this condition. The Kalamas of Kesaputta tell the Buddha that many teachers pass through their town, each praising his own view and disparaging others, and they are left in doubt. The Buddha's advice, as the sutta records it, is not to settle the matter by rumour, tradition, scripture quoted at them, logical speculation, appearances, or the prestige of the speaker. Instead they are to examine for themselves whether a given state leads to harm and suffering or to welfare and happiness, and whether the wise would praise or blame it.

The sutta then names the roots to inspect: greed, hatred and delusion on the one side, non-greed, non-hatred and non-delusion on the other. Greed, it observes, leads a person to take life, steal and lie, and to lead others the same way. This is not a moral lecture so much as a diagnostic method. Before acting, look at the motive beneath the action.

For a leader, an investor or a founder, this is practical. When a listing is oversubscribed fivefold and everyone is certain, ask which root is driving the certainty. When a summit communique soothes, ask whether it reflects non-hatred or merely the pause between hostilities. When fear urges you to sell everything or to defend a position at all costs, notice whether delusion is steering.

The same sutta cautions against the opposite error. It does not tell the Kalamas to doubt everything. It points to the test of results: what, when undertaken and observed, leads to long-term welfare for oneself and others. A digital nomad choosing a base, a fund manager sizing a position, a minister drafting a trade text can all ask the same two questions: does this increase suffering, and would the wise approve?

There is also a quiet encouragement. The sutta describes the person whose mind is free of enmity, ill will and corruption as dwelling with a heart filled with goodwill, and as having found reassurance in the present life regardless of outcomes. Markets will move and summits will conclude. Your steadiness comes from the quality of the mind that meets them.

Today, before the next decision, pause for three breaths and name the root beneath it. That small inquiry is a form of independence that no headline can price.

Personal Reflection

Today's headlines are loud with certainty: record valuations, confident declarations, firm denials. Notice how much of that certainty you absorbed before breakfast, and how much of it quietly became yours.

Most of us are asked daily to hold opinions about things we cannot verify. The cost is subtle: attention spent on borrowed convictions is attention not spent on the life in front of you, the people you love and the work only you can do.
