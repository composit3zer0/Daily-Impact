Dhamma Corner

In an era defined by hyper-accelerated capital flows, silicon bottlenecks, and geopolitical brinkmanship, leaders and innovators constantly feel the pressure to outpace change. Markets surge and retreat on a single earnings report; supply chains fracture across continents; digital nomads pivot across time zones seeking refuge or opportunity. Yet this relentless striving exposes a fundamental vulnerability: the unexamined assumption that security can be engineered purely through external control.

Early Buddhist psychology diagnoses this exact turbulence as the consequence of leaning into impermanence (üniccā) while demanding permanence. When the mind treats volatile conditions—such as fluctuating bond yields, memory component shortages, or shifting alliances—as solid, permanent realities, suffering and anxiety inevitably follow. The Buddha repeatedly emphasized that conditioned phenomena are characterized by change, unsatisfactoriness, and non-self. To seek lasting refuge in external systems is akin to building a fortress on shifting sand.

For the modern strategist or decision-maker, inner clarity does not require retreating from the marketplace or abandoning innovation. Rather, it demands developing the capacity for radical non-reactivity amidst rapid transformation. By observing market fluctuations, technological disruptions, and geopolitical tensions with mindful equanimity (upekkhā), one acts with precision and compassion rather than frantic desperation. True mastery begins not by subduing the entire external world, but by thoroughly understanding and steadying the mind that perceives it.
